
\section{RQ1: Do current adaptation methods generalise, or memorise?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Adapting a code model to obfuscated input works, and works easily. Tuning on output prediction over
obfuscated programs gains roughly \textbf{19 points} over the untuned model on the transforms it was
trained on. The question this paper asks is what that number is made of. Adaptation can raise
forward accuracy two ways: by teaching the model to \emph{read} the program better, or by teaching
it the surface a particular transform leaves behind. Only the first deserves to be called
robustness.

We separate them with two tests. Both ask the same question --- does the competence survive leaving
the distribution it was fit to? --- and they share no machinery.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, topsep=2pt, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Composition.} Real obfuscators stack transforms. Does competence on single
        transforms survive being stacked with a transform family the model has never seen?
  \item \textbf{Reversal.} Does it survive the task being run backwards --- given a return value,
        produce a call that yields it? No adapter is ever trained on this direction, so any
        backward gain is transferred program understanding rather than task fitting.
\end{itemize}

We apply both to the field's existing answers: inference-time routing over per-transform
specialists, weight-space merging of those specialists, and simply training on a breadth of
transforms. These are baselines, not proposals. Breadth fails both tests; routing tracks breadth wherever the
two are measured together and inherits its verdict; merging composes less than breadth on seen stacks
and, unlike breadth, keeps what it composes when an unseen family enters.

\subsection{The composition test}

Routing composes exactly as far as breadth does, and on CodeLlama-7B merging barely composes
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1_composition}). On the ten stacks of seen transforms, a learned router over
per-transform specialists beats a \emph{random} gate by $+1.34$ [$+0.48$, $+2.17$] and a fixed uniform
gate by $+1.40$ [$+0.52$, $+2.25$]: the mixture is worth the whole gain ($+19.79$ over the untuned model)
and the routing about a point of it. A hard argmax router is indistinguishable from the soft one
($+0.03$ [$-0.13$, $+0.19$]), so a stacked input, which contains two mechanisms, gives the mixture no
blending advantage over picking one expert. And the router matches breadth training, $-0.28$
[$-1.57$, $+1.09$]; it neither adds to nor subtracts from what one adapter trained on the same data
learns. Task-vector merging is worse: the best merge (DARE-TIES) matches the clean-code control,
$+0.52$ [$-0.15$, $+1.20$], TIES and the two clean-anchored merges fall $1.7$ to $5.5$ points below
it, and DARE-linear collapses ($-20.56$), while breadth stands $+3.67$ [$+2.02$, $+5.24$] above the
same control.

Merging, however, is not simply a failed baseline once it is measured across the panel
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1_merge_panel}). On three models the best merge (DARE-TIES of the clean adapter and
the five specialists) sits \emph{above} the clean-code control on stacks of seen transforms ($+0.83$,
$+1.57$, $+2.31$; all intervals exclude zero) and $2.3$ to $4.0$ points below the anchored objective
there. On stacks that contain the unseen family it keeps its gain: it is above breadth on three of
three models ($+2.35$, $+2.20$, $+3.09$) and, against anchoring, below on CodeLlama-7B ($-1.39$),
level on Llama-3.1-8B ($+0.81$ [$-0.47$, $+2.12$]) and above on Granite ($+2.98$ [$+1.28$, $+4.65$]).
Merging never pays breadth's tax because it never had breadth's seen-stack gain; it is the cheap,
training-free strategy that composes a little and composes it everywhere. The pre-registered
expectation that merging would not beat the control on seen stacks was refuted on Llama-3.1-8B and
inconclusive on the other two.

Training breadth is the one that appears to work, and it is the interesting case. It composes on
stacks built from transforms it saw --- and the advantage evaporates the moment a stack contains a
family it did not. We state this as a \emph{difference} rather than as two separate levels, because
one interval being positive while another is negative is not a test of the difference between them,
and on four of the six models the unseen-side interval straddles zero on its own.

\input{tables/rq1_dissociation}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1_dissociation} is the core measurement. Breadth's advantage over clean-code tuning
is \textbf{2.7 to 5.3 points larger} on stacks built from seen transforms than on stacks containing
an unseen family, and the gap is significant on \textbf{every one of the six models} we could
measure it on, spanning four model lineages. What breadth buys is recombination of what the training
data covered, bounded exactly by that coverage.

\subsection{The reversal test}

The second test is independent of the first and gives the same answer. We evaluate the same
adapters, on the same programs, asked backwards, and grade by execution: a prediction is correct if
the produced call actually returns the gold value.

\input{tables/direction_ratio}

Table~\ref{tab:direction_ratio} reports the \emph{direction ratio} --- backward gain divided by
forward gain, both measured against the untuned model. A negative ratio means the arm bought forward
accuracy by \emph{losing} backward competence on the very same programs. \textbf{Every method that
fits the forward task directly is negative}: the format-only control at $-0.30$, clean-code tuning
at $-0.08$, breadth at $-0.05$. At most a tenth of any forward gain survives the flip, and for these
arms the surviving fraction is negative.

\subsection{What the two tests agree on}

A model that had learned to read obfuscated programs would keep that competence when the program is
stacked with something new, and when the question is asked from the other end. These adapters keep
neither. Both tests point at the same explanation: what is acquired is competence at \emph{this
task on these surfaces}. Section~\ref{sec:rq2} establishes what that surface is.
