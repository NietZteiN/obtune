We train one low-rank specialist per obfuscating transform and combine the specialists into a single
adapter before inference. The resulting system occupies the same design point as a router over
experts --- per-transform specialists, composed --- but resolves the composition offline, so the
deployed artefact is one adapter of the base architecture, requiring no gate, no expert bank, and no
additional computation at inference time. This section states the algorithm exactly as we run it.

\subsection{Specialists}
For each trainable condition $c \in \mathcal{C}$ we train one LoRA adapter on that condition's rows
alone, holding the base weights $W_0$ frozen. Adapter $c$ is the pair $(A_c, B_c)$ with
$A_c \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, $B_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, and it applies the update
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta_c \;=\; s_c\, B_c A_c , \qquad s_c \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{r},
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
%
where $s_c$ is the LoRA scaling. All specialists share a rank, an $\alpha$, a target-module set, an
optimiser and a step budget, so they differ only in training data. We merge the $n = |\mathcal{C}|$
specialists for $\mathcal{C} = \{\texttt{L0},\texttt{L1b},\texttt{L1r},\texttt{L2},\texttt{S1},
\texttt{S2}\}$, which is exactly the set of conditions pooled training sees.

\subsection{Sign-Consensus Merging}
TIES~\cite{ties} and DARE~\cite{dare} were both introduced for \emph{task vectors}: the difference
$\theta_{\mathrm{ft}} - \theta_{\mathrm{pre}}$ between a fine-tuned and a pre-trained model, in the
sense of task arithmetic~\cite{taskarithmetic}. Given a set of task tensors $\{T_c\}_{c=1}^{n}$ and
combining weights $\{w_c\}$, both proceed in three stages.

\paragraph{1. Sparsify.} Each tensor is reduced to a fraction $\rho$ of its entries. TIES keeps the
largest by magnitude,
%
\begin{equation}
\widehat{T}_c \;=\; T_c \odot \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\lvert T_c\rvert \geq \mathrm{topk}_{\rho}(\lvert T_c\rvert)\,\right],
\label{eq:trim}
\end{equation}
%
while DARE drops entries at random and rescales the survivors so the tensor is preserved in
expectation,
%
\begin{equation}
\widehat{T}_c \;=\; \frac{1}{\rho}\, T_c \odot M_c , \qquad M_c \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho).
\label{eq:dare}
\end{equation}
%
\paragraph{2. Elect a sign.} A single sign is chosen per coordinate by summing the sparsified tensors
and taking the sign of the sum, so that coordinates with large-magnitude support dominate:
%
\begin{equation}
\gamma \;=\; \mathrm{sign}\!\left( \sum_{c=1}^{n} \widehat{T}_c \right),
\qquad
m_c \;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, \mathrm{sign}(\widehat{T}_c) = \gamma \,\right].
\label{eq:elect}
\end{equation}
%
This is the \texttt{total} variant of the majority-sign rule, which weights by summed magnitude; the
alternative \texttt{frequency} variant counts signs instead. We use \texttt{total} throughout.

\paragraph{3. Disjoint merge.} Only the entries agreeing with the elected sign contribute, and the
sum is normalised by how many specialists contributed at that coordinate rather than by $n$:
%
\begin{equation}
T^{\star} \;=\; \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{n} w_c\, \widehat{T}_c \odot m_c}{\max\!\left(\sum_{c=1}^{n} m_c,\; 1\right)} .
\label{eq:disjoint}
\end{equation}
%
A coordinate on which every specialist opposes the consensus receives zero rather than an undefined
value, which is what the clamp in the denominator provides. \textsc{TIES} applies
Eq.~\ref{eq:trim}; \textsc{DARE-TIES} applies Eq.~\ref{eq:dare}; both then apply
Eqs.~\ref{eq:elect}--\ref{eq:disjoint}. Neither involves any training.

\subsection{Application to LoRA Adapters}
\label{sec:factorspace}
The procedure above is defined on task tensors, and a LoRA adapter does not store one: it stores the
factors $(A_c, B_c)$ whose product is the update. Our implementation, which follows the standard
PEFT path for LoRA, therefore treats the two factor sets as \emph{separate} collections of task
tensors and merges each independently:
%
\begin{equation}
A_{\star} \;=\; \mathrm{Merge}\!\left(\{A_c\},\,\{\sqrt{w_c s_c}\}\right),
\qquad
B_{\star} \;=\; \mathrm{Merge}\!\left(\{B_c\},\,\{\sqrt{w_c s_c}\}\right),
\label{eq:factormerge}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathrm{Merge}$ is Eqs.~\ref{eq:trim}--\ref{eq:disjoint} and the combining weight is split as a
square root across the two sides so that the product recovers $w_c s_c$. The merged adapter is
$(A_{\star}, B_{\star})$, written at the original rank $r$ with $\alpha = r$ so that its own scaling
is unity and the factor already carries the specialists' scaling. It is served by the same multi-LoRA
path as any single specialist.

Equation~\ref{eq:factormerge} is \emph{not} equivalent to applying $\mathrm{Merge}$ to the assembled
updates $\{\Delta_c\}$, and the inequivalence is structural. Each entry of an update is a sum over
the rank,
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta_c[m,n] \;=\; s_c \sum_{t=1}^{r} B_c[m,t]\, A_c[t,n],
\label{eq:ranksum}
\end{equation}
%
so no entry of $A_c$ or $B_c$ corresponds to a single coordinate of $\Delta_c$: an entry of $B_c$
participates in an entire row of the update and an entry of $A_c$ in an entire column. The sign
elected in Eq.~\ref{eq:elect} is therefore a statement about a factor coordinate, not about a
parameter of the update, and the two merges compute different quantities.
Figure~\ref{fig:factorspace} contrasts them. The gap is not a rounding effect: for random factors
with $r = 4$, $n = 3$ and $\rho = 0.5$, $B_{\star}A_{\star}$ and $\mathrm{Merge}(\{B_cA_c\})$ have a
cosine similarity of $0.40$ and disagree in sign on $39\%$ of coordinates.

We merge in factor space because the alternative introduces a confound. Recovering a rank-$r$ adapter
from a merged $\Delta^{\star}$ requires a truncated SVD, which changes the adapter's rank and
introduces reconstruction error \emph{before} the merging rule is evaluated; the deployed artefact
would no longer match the base architecture, and any measured effect would combine the merge with its
re-extraction. Factor-space merging preserves the rank exactly. The consequence for interpretation is
that our results characterise sign-consensus merging \emph{as it is applied to LoRA adapters in
practice}, not the published task-vector algorithm in its original setting; whether the two differ in
downstream accuracy is an open question these experiments do not address.

\subsection{Configuration}
We report two variants, \textsc{TIES} (Eq.~\ref{eq:trim}) and \textsc{DARE-TIES}
(Eq.~\ref{eq:dare}), both with density $\rho = 0.5$, the \texttt{total} majority-sign rule, and
uniform weights $w_c = 1/n$. Non-uniform weighting is an additional degree of freedom; holding the
weights uniform preserves the comparison against pooled training --- which likewise weights its
constituent conditions equally by construction --- as a comparison of composition strategy rather
than of tuning effort. Density was selected by a sweep over $\rho \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ on a
1.5\,B pilot model outside the evaluation panel and applied unchanged to all eight panel models, so
it is neither fitted to the panel nor tuned for it. \textsc{DARE-TIES} is stochastic through
Eq.~\ref{eq:dare}; we fix its seed and record it with the merged adapter, and each merge is performed
once in \texttt{float32}.
