Our approach trains one low-rank specialist per obfuscating transform and merges the specialists into
a single adapter before inference (Figure~\ref{fig:merging}). It occupies the same design point as a
router system --- per-transform specialists, composed --- but resolves the composition \emph{before}
inference, so what is deployed is a single adapter of exactly the base architecture, with no gate, no
expert bank and no run-time cost over ordinary fine-tuning.

\paragraph{What is combined.} Specialist $i$ contributes the update its training produced,
$\Delta_i = B_i A_i$, with $B_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A_i \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$.
Naive averaging of such updates degrades as their number grows, because entries that disagree cancel.
TIES attributes this to two forms of interference --- redundant entries carrying little signal, and
outright sign disagreement on a parameter --- and removes both in three steps: \emph{trim} each update
to its largest-magnitude entries, \emph{elect} a sign per parameter by summing signed magnitudes
across specialists, and \emph{merge} only the entries whose sign matches the elected
one~\cite{ties}. DARE prepends a sparsification of a different kind: drop a fraction $p$ of each
update's entries at random and rescale the survivors by $1/(1-p)$, preserving the update in
expectation while cutting its redundancy~\cite{dare}. Nothing is trained; both are arithmetic on
weights already learned, in the task-vector sense established by task arithmetic~\cite{taskarithmetic}.

\paragraph{Where it is applied, and why that is not the same thing.} We merge in \emph{LoRA factor
space}: the trim--elect--merge rule is applied independently to the set of $A$ factors and to the set
of $B$ factors, and the merged adapter is $B_{\mathrm{merged}} A_{\mathrm{merged}}$, with each
specialist's combining weight split as $\sqrt{w_i}$ on either side so the product recovers $w_i$. This
is the standard implementation for LoRA and it is \emph{not} identical to applying TIES to the
assembled updates $\{\Delta_i\}$: sign election on the factors is not sign election on their product.
The difference is not negligible. On random factors at $r = 4$ with three specialists and density
$0.5$, $B_{\mathrm{merged}} A_{\mathrm{merged}}$ and $\mathrm{TIES}(\{B_i A_i\})$ have cosine
similarity $0.40$ and disagree in sign on $39\%$ of entries.

We merge in factor space nonetheless, and the reason is the alternative. Assembling $\Delta_i$,
merging, and re-extracting a low-rank adapter requires an SVD truncation that changes the rank and
introduces reconstruction error \emph{before} the merging rule is evaluated --- so the deployed
adapter would differ from the base architecture and the measured effect would confound the merge with
its re-extraction. Factor-space merging preserves the rank exactly and leaves the result servable as
any other adapter. The consequence for interpretation is that our results are evidence about
sign-consensus merging \emph{as it is actually applied to LoRA adapters}, which is how practitioners
use it, rather than about the published task-vector algorithm in its original setting.

\paragraph{Weighting.} The specialists are combined with uniform weights. A weighted merge is a tuning
knob, and leaving it uniform keeps the comparison against pooled training --- which also weights its
conditions equally by construction --- a comparison of \emph{composition}, not of tuning effort.
